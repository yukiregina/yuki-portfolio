Transactional Journey

A large direct-sales company introduced biometric anti-fraud validation for resellers' first in-person credit purchase. It was breaking queues, cancelling orders, and contradicting the brand's hospitality promise. I ran the full research cycle: two months of fieldwork across the whole journey.

The brief I was handed: find out why biometrics is broken.

The brief I shipped: proof that biometrics wasn't the problem.

Context

A new anti-fraud strategy met an old tension

The company's reseller spaces run on hospitality: the first visit should feel like a gift, not a checkpoint. The anti-fraud logic was sound; the implementation did the opposite, pulling staff off the sales floor.

My Role

Product designer running the full research cycle

The research scope spanned Risk, Fraud, Reseller Platform, Experience, and Operations.

Before: biometrics at the cashier, blocking every order. After: biometrics at store entry, clearing the flow.

Research Approach

Six methods across two months

Three angles on one journey: as designed, as lived in the store, and as experienced by the reseller.

Cross-team session to map systems and surface known pain points before fieldwork.

The central reframe: biometrics is not the problem. The in-person process experience is.

3 Root Areas

The problem was distributed, not isolated

Each root area had its own actors, systems, and failure modes.

01. Registration: fragmented flow, no standardisation, staff with no proactive visibility

02. Biometrics: 5–10 min per reseller at the cashier, blocking queues and cancelling orders

03. Communication: staff learning about requirements at payment, not before the reseller arrived

Systems Involved

12 systems, zero single owner

No single team owned the journey. The flow crossed 12 interconnected systems, each with its own owner, roadmap, and definition of "done."

Risk Platform Reseller Platform Reseller App Super App Biometric System A Biometric System B Extranet Internal Portal Store Dashboard External Credit Bureaus WhatsApp Risk Engine

Recommendations

Where to act, and in what order

Prioritised by effort and impact.

01

Move biometrics to store entry Primary direction

A QR code or visual prompt at the entrance starts the process before item selection, while the reseller is still relaxed. This single shift resolves the queues and the cancellation loop, and keeps the hospitality promise without removing any anti-fraud protection.

02

Give staff real-time biometric visibility

A status flag on the reseller profile in the store dashboard lets staff see, before the cashier, whether biometrics is done.

03

Redesign the communication touchpoints Primary direction

Replace text-heavy process updates with visual, step-by-step materials that work digitally and printed. Communicate the requirement before the reseller arrives, not at the cashier.

04

Adapt the transactional policy for in-person context

Online and in-person risk policies were identical, though one context is anonymous and the other supervised. Work with Risk on a separate in-person rule set.

What the Research Changed

The decision was not to change the onboarding.

The team decided not to redesign the onboarding: the research showed the friction lived elsewhere. Preventing the wrong investment is an outcome too.

From Findings to Direction

A blueprint to find where the real opportunity was.

With the UX research team, I built a service blueprint of the full journey. My input: move biometrics into the app, so resellers complete it before arriving at the store. That became the leading direction for the next cycle.

Key Learnings

What this project taught me

  • When a problem has no single owner, the research output has to work as a shared language: a map everyone can point at and agree on.
  • The loudest pain point (biometrics is broken) was covering a quieter one: an online-first risk logic applied to a human, in-person relationship. Finding that reframe was the real contribution.
